# Lesson 8: Writing to an expert you have never met

Technical work gets read when the first message earns it. This lesson covers how to write a short message to a senior engineer, using the draft in notes/outreach.md. Nothing has been sent.

## 1. What the reader's time costs

A Head of Cryogenic Refuelling receives messages from students, vendors and recruiters. They decide in about ten seconds. The message must answer three questions at once: who are you, what did you do, and what do you want from me. If any is missing, it is deleted.

## 2. The structure that works

1. One line of who you are.
2. One or two lines of what you built, with one number.
3. One line saying where it is weak. This is what makes an expert trust you.
4. A small ask: 15 minutes or a pointer, not a job.
5. An attachment short enough to read in five minutes.

Compare with a weak message: "I am passionate about space and would love to work for TEC." It has no work, no number and no ask the reader can say yes to.

## 3. Why include the weakness

An expert will find the flaw anyway. If you state it first ("no LOX or LCH4 test data behind it"), you look honest and you show that you know the field. If they find it first, the whole message loses credibility. In this project the weakness is real: validation is hydrogen and nitrogen only. Say it in one sentence.

## 4. One number, chosen carefully

The draft uses: "5 to 10 K of inlet subcooling decides whether receiver chilldown is needed." Why this number and not the 0.4 %? Because it is a finding the reader can argue with from their own experience, which invites a reply. A loss percentage is something they can just nod at. Check the choice against lesson 5: the claim is a trend, which is the robust result, not a threshold, which is optimistic.

## 5. Claims you must not make

- "Validated for LOX and LCH4." It is not.
- Anything about TEC's own design or plans. You do not know them.
- Precise thresholds as facts. Use "in the model".

## 6. The public post

A post is for a wider audience, so lead with a question the audience cares about, give three findings, then one sentence on how far to trust it, and ask for data. Keep the figure simple: one panel. Do not publish until you can defend every number (lesson 7). The repository stays private until you decide otherwise.

## 7. Worked example

The draft's 100-word message contains: who (final-year student, Bristol), work (Python model, validated against NASA hydrogen tests), one finding (5 to 10 K subcooling), ask (15 minutes on where it is wrong), attachment (short note). Count the words: about 85. Each sentence maps to a numbered item in section 2. It leaves out the 7 % and 26 % error figures because the attached note carries them; the longer email version includes them.

## Self-test questions

1. What three questions must a cold message answer?
2. Why state a weakness in the first message?
3. Why choose the subcooling finding over the 0.4 % loss as the headline?
4. Name two claims you must not make.
5. Why should the ask be small?

## Answers

1. Who you are, what you did, and what you want.
2. It builds trust and shows you know the field; the expert would find it anyway.
3. It is a trend an expert can discuss from experience, and trends are the robust result of the model, while thresholds are optimistic.
4. That the model is validated for LOX or LCH4, and anything about TEC's design.
5. A small ask is easy to say yes to; a job request is easy to ignore.
